Attendance UX proposal

The Attendance Register

A visual redesign for a teacher-first, record-everything attendance system — built around one idea: the app is a register, not an inbox. Nothing waits for approval; everything is written down, plainly, the moment it happens.

Four decisions that shape everything below

Design principles

Every screen that follows is a direct consequence of these four calls. Where a mockup does something non-obvious, it traces back to one of these.

01

Show why, not just what

A hidden button looks broken. A disabled button with a reason ("142m away — move closer") teaches the rule instead of hiding it.

02

Ledger, not inbox

History is a row per day, colour-coded, done. No item waits for anyone to act. Nothing has an unread count.

03

Numbers before tables

The Principal's first screen is four counts, not a grid. Tables are for the second click, not the first glance.

04

Investigation is opt-in

The full activity log exists, but it's a second-tier tab — for the rare moment someone asks "what actually happened," not daily use.

1 · Out of range

The check-in window is open, but the teacher is 410m away. The button is present but disabled — not gone — with the exact distance and the exact threshold shown.

Why not hide it? Your spec says the button shouldn't be shown when out of range. A dead patch of screen where a button used to be reads as a bug to someone on a basic Android phone with patchy signal. Showing it disabled, with the live distance counting down as they walk, turns the rule into feedback instead of a mystery — same enforcement, less confusion. The backend still re-checks location independently either way.

3 · Working, reminder active

Once checked in, the whole home screen is just today's fact and the check-out button. The reminder appears as a calm inline banner starting 15 minutes before closing — not a push notification that interrupts a class — and it vanishes the instant they check out.

Why a banner, not a popup? A modal alarm during teaching hours is disruptive and gets dismissed on reflex. An inline banner is seen the next time they glance at their phone, which is exactly the urgency level this actually has. A quieter duplicate goes to the notification tray for anyone who's left the app entirely, on the same 15-before / 30-after schedule, spaced a few minutes apart rather than every minute.

4 · Monthly history

A colour-coded week strip sits above the list — a teacher can see the shape of their month (mostly green, one amber day) before reading a single row. The list below is the detail: exact times, exact distances, exact status. Missing-checkout gets amber, not red — it's a recorded fact, not a violation.

Why a strip and a list, not just one? The strip answers "how's my month going" in half a second. The list answers "what exactly happened on the 11th." Neither replaces the other — a calendar grid alone hides the numbers, a list alone hides the pattern.

Principal / admin side

Reports first, investigation second

There is no queue to clear. The Principal's day starts with numbers, not a to-do list, and drills down only when something actually needs a look.

5 · Dashboard — today, in four numbers

This is the landing screen. No table, no list of names, no items marked "pending." Four counts, each one a live filter into Reports. A Principal with 40 teachers and one free minute between periods gets the whole picture at a glance.

Why not lead with a table? A table asks to be read row by row. A stat card asks to be glanced at. The first screen of the day should answer "is anything unusual today" in under two seconds — the table is for once that answer is yes.

6 · Reports — the pattern lives in the colour

Zero stays quiet grey text. Anything above the pattern threshold turns into a solid amber pill — Abhinav's "6" for missing checkout is immediately the thing your eye lands on in that row, before you've read a single label.

Why colour the count, not the row? Colouring the whole row turns every teacher with one late day into an alarm. Colouring just the number that crossed the threshold keeps the signal proportional to how serious it actually is.

7 · Teacher drill-down — same register, admin lens

Clicking a name in Reports replaces the table with this teacher's own month — the same strip-plus-list layout the teacher sees, so the Principal is reading the identical record, not a separate "admin view" of it.

Why "Correct" instead of a review queue? There's no longer a list of things demanding action. Every day is just a row; the correction tool lives on the row itself, collapsed, for the Principal to open only on the specific day they've decided to look at.

8 · Settings — grouped, collapsed

Twelve-plus fields no longer sit in one flat wall. Four named groups, each collapsed except the one you're editing — the same fields, reorganised by what a Principal is actually trying to change in one sitting.

9 · Activity log — a second tab, not the front door

Every login, check-in/out, blocked attempt, correction, and settings change is written here — who, what, when, where, result — but it's tucked under its own tab, reverse-chronological, filterable by teacher and event type. It's for the day someone asks "what actually happened on Aug 8," not for daily use.

Why hide it at all if everything's logged? Logging everything and surfacing everything are different goals. A feed this dense on the main screen would drown the four numbers that actually matter day to day.

This has to survive year three, not just week one

Designing for a large school and a long history

Every mockup above used three or four rows to stay readable. None of it works if it silently assumes a small school and a short memory — a real deployment means dozens to hundreds of teachers, and years of daily rows piling up underneath. Three specific parts break first, so they're designed for scale from the start rather than patched later.

10 · Reports scales by count, not by row height

The mockup earlier showed 3 teachers — this is the same table at 84. Nothing about the layout changes; the table just paginates, 25 rows at a time, with the count stated plainly.

What actually has to change underneath: the earlier version implied fetching every teacher's full day-by-day records just to show four summary counts — fine for 5 teachers, expensive for 200. The server needs to compute and send only the summary counts for the visible page; a teacher's full daily detail is fetched once, only when that one row is clicked. Search and the "only needing a look" filter move from filtering an already-loaded list to filtering the query itself, so page 1 of a search is fast whether the school has 20 teachers or 2,000.

11 · Trend, not six separate months

The drill-down from earlier only ever showed the current month. Whether a pattern is getting better or worse over a school year is a real question a Principal will actually ask — this answers it in one glance instead of six page loads.

Why 6 months and not the full year or two? A trend needs enough points to show direction without becoming its own dense table to read. Six months is a term-length window; going further back is one more click into a specific month, not a wall of bars nobody scans anyway.

12 · The log that grows forever, scoped so it never has to

Every event, every teacher, every day — over a couple of years this is the one table with no natural ceiling. It opens scoped to last 7 days by default, never "everything." Widening the range or the filters is a deliberate click, not the starting state.

Why not just paginate the whole thing? Pagination alone still means the very first query has to search the entire history to find the newest 20 rows once there are millions of them. A default date range keeps that query cheap by construction — this is a backend indexing decision as much as a UI one (index the log by school+date and by teacher+date, so both the default view and a "show me this one teacher's full history" search stay fast years in).
